# Internal linking strategy: stitch your website together

You may already have the answer a visitor needs somewhere on your website. The problem is that the page they found never points to it. Internal linking joins those pieces: an explanation to a detailed guide, a guide to a relevant service, a service to the case study that shows the work. I call it stitching together all the pages of your site with text links and targets and anchors.

## Make existing work easier to find

A website accumulates knowledge over years. Articles arrive in the blog, services sit in another section, and older pages disappear from the paths a visitor normally follows. A linking strategy looks across that whole collection and asks which page would help somebody next. That can expose useful connections without commissioning another batch of articles or waiting for another website to link to yours.

## What I examine
- **The destinations that matter:** important services, products, categories, guides, and supporting evidence.
- **Existing routes:** navigation, breadcrumbs, body links, related content, and links generated by templates.
- **Missing connections:** relevant pages with few useful incoming links and pages found in exports but absent from the crawl.
- **The wording:** whether the visible anchor text explains the destination in the sentence around it.
- **The targets:** live canonical URLs, working section anchors, and links still aimed at redirects or retired pages.

## A plan an editor can implement

The deliverable is a source and destination map. Each proposed link names the page where it belongs, the paragraph or section that gives it context, the target URL, and suggested anchor text. I group template work separately from individual editorial additions so your developer and your content editor each get an actionable list. Where authorized, I can make the additions in the CMS or through a reviewed repository change.

## Choose a useful path, then a useful phrase

The link should earn its place in the reader's journey. A page about a missing product description might lead to an explanation of rendering, while a migration checklist might point to a redirect worksheet. The destination decides the wording. Repeating a search phrase across unrelated sentences makes the page harder to read and does not substitute for a relevant connection.

## When the link exists only after interaction

In my [event platform investigation](https://christopherabraham.com/case-studies/event-app-links/), the way an application exposed its routes affected discovery. That is why a linking review includes the HTML and rendering behavior, not just the words an editor sees. A visible card, a search box, and a normal text link can give crawlers different ways into the same content. I check the actual path.

## Measure the work in two stages

First I verify that the agreed links are present, readable, and aimed at the intended pages or sections. A fresh crawl shows changes to incoming links, discovery, and depth. Then I monitor the destination pages' search observations over an agreed period. The implementation record and the later search results stay separate, so a published link is never reported as a ranking gain before there is evidence.

## Internal linking questions

### Can you work with the pages I already have?

Yes. Existing content is the starting point. If a proposed destination does not answer a useful next question, I flag the content gap rather than forcing a link to an unsuitable page.

### Can this be a focused project?

Yes. A review can cover a particular service, a group of related guides, an ecommerce category, or the whole site. The agreed inventory sets the scope.
