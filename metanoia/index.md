# Online Perception Management Begins Where Cleanup Ends

Every reputation engagement I accept starts with one condition, stated before any contract and repeated until it lands: the client has to change first. Online perception management, the proactive half of my practice, only works on a client who has turned around. Cleanup sold to a client who has not turned is a subscription to a problem that renews itself.

I wrote the condition down in 2013, in an essay titled "Reputation management is not about dressing up mistakes": "If you want to fix your online reputation, writing big checks to an online reputation agency isn't enough over the long haul—you'll also really need to get your own house in order." Thirteen years on, that sentence is still the whole position. The checks are sometimes necessary. They are never sufficient.

## Cleanup is real work with a hard ceiling

[Online reputation management](https://chrisabraham.com/orm/), ORM, is the reactive discipline: defensive SEO against what is already published. Some of it is straightforward and finite. A client who has been lied about, or hit by a grudge with a byline, or buried under a namesake's record, presents the scenario I call "the clean": take back the land ceded to negative reviews and terrible happenstance, restore the first pages of search to something accurate, and the job ends. I take those cases gladly, and I have taken them since 2002.

The other scenario I call "repair and repeat." The client's own conduct keeps generating the material I am hired to bury. I described it in the same 2013 essay: "Online reputation management does work, but it's an endless game of whack-a-mole." Then I gave the economics their own section, "The law of diminishing returns": brute force works for a while, "but there will be a point of diminishing returns as Google normalizes the online reputation management campaign and the newest, freshest, and most relevant real, organic, authentic, and popular content will bubble to the top." A client who keeps feeding the record pays more every year for less ground than the year before. No retainer outruns that arithmetic for long.

## An apology is a tool, not a turnaround

When a client has genuinely wronged people in public, the first move is not content. It is contrition, owned out loud and in the client's own name. I named the extreme form of it in 2013, in a [Biznology essay on mea maxima culpa marketing](https://biznology.com/2013/02/mea-maxima-culpa-marketing/): accept responsibility fully, show honest contrition, and have enough grace to be joshed with afterward. Done honestly, it disarms. As I put it to Argentum in 2019: "It's 'mea maxima culpa marketing.' You show you're flexible, you listen."

Done as theater, it curdles, and I said that in 2013 too: a plan built on performed contrition makes things worse, much worse. Two standing rules from that [2019 Argentum interview on protecting your online reputation](https://www.argentum.org/expert-shares-best-practices-to-protect-your-online-reputation/) govern here: "Your reputation online can be curated, but it has to be the truth," and "Online reputation damage is like water damage. It'll seep through if you try to paper it over." An apology pasted over unchanged conduct is paper over water damage, and the seep always wins.

## The turnaround has a name

The word I use for the change itself is metanoia: the Greek word for a change of mind that shows up as a change of direction. I argued the substance in print in 2013, with the house-in-order line above, and I held the same position through my years at Reputation.com, where I was Team Lead, Special Projects, and in my own practice since. Only recently did I start calling the turn by its proper name. A client in metanoia has stopped producing the evidence. From that point my work changes character: it stops being suppression and becomes documentation, making the changed conduct findable in the client's own name, in the places search engines and answer engines read first.

## Perception management is the proactive half

[Online perception management](https://chrisabraham.com/services/online-perception-management-services), OPM, is what becomes possible after the turn. It builds the record before the record is needed: owned pages, claimed profiles, and published work that state plainly who the client is and what the client does now. The reason is a sentence I gave Argentum: "Google abhors a vacuum. When a community doesn't own its narrative, its narrative is vulnerable." A thin record invites the worst surviving fragment to stand for the whole person. A tended one gives a searcher, and now a summarizing machine, something accurate to find first.

None of this is a blitz. Reputation, I told Argentum, is like getting fitter: "Spend about 20 minutes a day, walk more, take the stairs at first. If you try too hard in a weekend, you might break something." Steady, truthful, and boring beats dramatic every time.

## The boundary of the condition

The condition has a boundary, and I keep it bright. It applies where the client's conduct is the source of the record. Where the record is simply false, the doctrine is the simpler one from 2013: "If you've simply been besmirched, write a check to me." Nobody owes a turnaround for something they never did. But where the conduct is real and still current, I will not sell suppression as a substitute for change, because I know precisely how that engagement ends: with the law of diminishing returns collecting its fee, forever.
