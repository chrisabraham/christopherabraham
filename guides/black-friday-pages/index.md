# Black Friday sale pages that keep their rankings

By [Christopher Abraham](https://christopherabraham.com/about/) · Published October 26, 2026

Every November I find shops publishing a brand-new Black Friday page at an address like /black-friday-2026, while last year's page sits forgotten at /black-friday-2025 with all the links people gave it. The new page starts with nothing, and Google has to find it, crawl it, and decide what it's worth in the few weeks that matter. One permanent address fixes that, and it's the advice Google itself has given.

## What Google has said

In July 2021, Alan Kent of Google's Search Relations team published a post on deal pages that restated the earlier Black Friday advice in a short list: create dedicated pages for Black Friday and Cyber Monday, reuse the same URL each year, create the pages early so Google can index them, link to the sale page from the home page, and include a high-quality image. The same post added that the page title should carry the event description, the page should have text that describes the event, and if the deals are revealed on the day, the page should go up early with general information and be recrawled after the update.

That post is five years old, and it's still the most specific official statement on sale pages I can find. The reasoning hasn't aged: a page that has existed for years has links, history, and a place in the index that a new page has to earn from scratch.

## One URL, updated every year

Choose an address with no year in it, such as /black-friday/ or /sale/black-friday/. Put the year in the title and the heading, where shoppers want to see it, and change it each autumn. The address stays put. If you have old yearly pages, redirect each one to the permanent address with a 301 so the links pointing at them still count.

Cyber Monday is a separate search with its own audience, so it can earn its own permanent page. A single combined page works too when the offers are identical. What fails is a new page per year per event, which turns one strong page into eight weak ones over four years.

## When to publish

Google Trends shows searches for Black Friday building for weeks before the day, every year. Publish the page, or refresh last year's, by early November. If you can't reveal the deals yet, say what's coming: which departments, when the sale opens, how to get notified. That gives Google something real to index. On the day the deals go live, update the page and request recrawling of that URL in Search Console's URL Inspection tool.

Link to the page from the home page and the main navigation while the sale runs. That link is the strongest signal you can send about which page matters this month.

## What goes on the page

Write a few paragraphs a shopper would read: what's on sale, the dates, shipping cutoffs for delivery before the holidays, the return window for gifts, and how the discount is applied. Then link to the actual sale products and categories. A page that's only a banner and a countdown clock has nothing for a search engine or an AI assistant to quote.

Prices belong on the product pages. If those pages carry merchant listing structured data, the sale price can appear in Google's shopping results. Check that the price in the markup matches the visible price on the day the sale starts; when they disagree, Google has two prices and no reason to trust either.

## After the sale

Don't delete the page in December, and don't redirect it to the home page. Keep it live with a short note that the sale has ended, a sentence about when it returns, an email signup if you have one, and links to current offers. Take it out of the main navigation, but leave it linked from somewhere on the site, such as a sales or offers hub, so it stays crawlable.

The page will rank for little in March. What matters is that in November it wakes up with years of history behind it instead of none.

## Measure it the same way each year

In Search Console, filter the Performance report to the page's URL and compare the same date window around Black Friday each year. Because the address never changes, the comparison is simple. That's another quiet benefit of one permanent URL: yearly pages make the history impossible to read. Keep a note of the dates the page was refreshed and the deals went live, so a jump or a dip can be traced to something that happened.

## Questions about holiday sale pages

### Should the year be in the title?

Yes. Shoppers search for "Black Friday 2026" and expect current deals. Put the year in the title and heading, and keep it out of the URL.

### What about other seasonal sales?

The same rule fits any sale that returns: a back-to-school page, a spring sale, an anniversary event. One permanent address per recurring event.

### My platform made a new page every year. What now?

Pick the strongest one, usually the oldest or the one with the most links, move it to a year-free address, and redirect the rest to it.
