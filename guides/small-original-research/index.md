# How to publish useful original research without a big budget

By [Christopher Abraham](https://christopherabraham.com/about/) · Published October 12, 2026

You do not need a national survey to contribute something worth reading. A carefully documented investigation of twenty pages can answer a practical question that a thousand recycled articles leave unresolved. The valuable part is the discipline: explain what you examined, make the method inspectable, and keep the conclusion within the evidence. I would rather publish a modest finding with clear boundaries than a sweeping statistic nobody can reproduce.

## Start with a question small enough to finish

“What makes websites successful?” is too broad for an afternoon. “Do these twenty public appointment pages expose their booking link in the initial HTML?” is manageable. It names the population, the thing being tested, and a result someone can check. The example is hypothetical; it is a research plan, not a claim about a completed study.

A useful question has a consequence. If the booking link only appears after a particular interaction, a publisher has something concrete to investigate. If the page exposes it immediately, that rules out one possible explanation for an access problem. Either result helps a reader decide what to test next.

Before collecting anything, write a sentence describing the decision the investigation could inform. Then list what it cannot establish. A check of visible links cannot measure appointment completion, prove crawler behavior, or explain a business's revenue. Those would require different evidence.

## Choose your sample before seeing the results

Write down how the pages enter the sample. Perhaps they are every appointment page in one public directory, or every qualifying page in a saved list. Record the selection date and the inclusion rule. Do not quietly replace awkward cases with examples that support the story you hoped to tell.

A convenient sample can still be informative, provided it is described honestly. Twenty websites chosen from your own bookmarks do not represent an industry. A test of your own archive describes that archive. Avoid dressing either one up as a random survey.

Keep a record of exclusions as well. If a site is unavailable, distinguish that from a site that fails the actual test. Report the denominator whenever you publish a proportion. Three failures among fifteen successfully tested pages is a different statement from three among twenty selected pages.

## Write a method another person could repeat

Decide what counts as a pass before starting. For the appointment example, the rule might require a normal link to a booking destination in the HTML response. Record whether redirects are followed, whether authentication is excluded, and whether a browser comparison is part of the procedure.

A method note should identify the tool and relevant version, the date, and any setting that could alter the result. Mobile and desktop rendering can differ. A consent choice can change loaded resources. An authenticated editor session can reveal content that a public visitor cannot see.

Save the observations in a simple ledger:
- **Sample identifier:** a stable label for each selected case.
- **Tested address:** the requested URL and final destination.
- **Observation time:** when the response or interaction was checked.
- **Result:** pass, fail, unavailable, or unresolved, using defined criteria.
- **Evidence:** the relevant response excerpt, output, or screenshot.

A screenshot can illustrate an observation, but it should not carry the whole method. Publish the important information as text so readers can inspect it without interpreting a tiny image. Remove credentials, personal information, and irrelevant private material before sharing any artifacts.

## Separate counting from explaining

Once the observations are recorded, calculate the result and check it against the ledger. Describe the count in ordinary language. If the sample is small, the exact numerator and denominator are often more informative than a percentage with several decimal places.

Then explain the limits. You might have established that some pages expose a link only after rendering. You have not established that this caused a search decline. Correlation between two observations does not identify the mechanism connecting them. A plausible explanation belongs in a clearly labeled interpretation section.

For a comparison, keep the environments as similar as possible and state what changed. If you test a page before and after a repair, retain both versions and the test settings. When several changes happen together, acknowledge that the result cannot isolate the contribution of each one.

## Publish the finding and its working notes together

The article should answer the research question near the top, then show how the answer was obtained. Include the selection rule, the method, the results, and the limitations. A downloadable table can help, but its definitions and main finding should remain on the page.

Use a permanent address and a descriptive title. Give the investigation a named author and an actual publication date. If you revise the method or correct a count, explain the correction and keep a visible revision note. Silent replacement makes it difficult for someone who already cited the finding to understand what happened.

Google's helpful content guidance asks publishers to consider originality, sourcing, and how material was produced. That supports making the work inspectable; it does not promise rankings for a small study. ## Use AI to assist the work, then check its output

An assistant can suggest a ledger structure, help write a test script, or identify inconsistent labels. Treat those contributions as drafts. Confirm that a script measures the stated condition, inspect a few cases manually, and reconcile every published number with the actual observations.

Do not ask a model to invent missing measurements or supply plausible quotations. If the evidence is incomplete, narrow the claim or repeat the test. If AI materially helped produce the research, describe its role where that context would help the reader evaluate the work.

## A first project worth attempting

Pick one recurring technical question and a small public sample. Reserve one session for defining the method, another for collecting observations, and a final session for checking the draft against the evidence. Publish only when the numbers, examples, and limitations agree.

The result may be a short, useful reference rather than a headline announcement. That is enough. A reader who can repeat your test has something more valuable than an unsupported prediction.
