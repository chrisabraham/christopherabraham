What happens to your old domains when they lapse

By Christopher Abraham · Published October 6, 2026

Every business that's been around a while has a few domains it stopped caring about: the old agency name, a product that was retired, a campaign site from years ago. When one of them expires, it doesn't quietly disappear. Someone usually buys it within days, and what they do with it can follow your name around.

A real example

While building this site, I checked the domain of a firm I co-founded years ago. Search results still showed its old team page with my bio. Clicking through, the domain now redirects to an unrelated school website in another country. Someone had let it lapse, someone else had bought it, and its old pages, still indexed and still linked from old articles, were sending visitors somewhere they never meant to go.

Why expired domains are valuable to strangers

An old domain carries history that a new one can't buy: years of backlinks from news sites, universities, and directories; an indexed footprint; and in some cases, remaining trust with search engines. That makes expired domains a commodity. Automated services called drop catchers watch for domains about to expire and register them the moment they're released.

What buyers typically do with them

  • Redirect them to another site to pass along the old links' value, often to unrelated businesses.
  • Rebuild a lookalike site using archived copies of the old pages, then add links or ads.
  • Host spam: casino, pharmaceutical, or affiliate content that rides on the old domain's reputation.
  • Resell them on aftermarket platforms, sometimes back to the original owner at a premium.
  • Receive email. If the new owner sets up mail, messages addressed to old accounts at the domain, including password resets for services you registered years ago, can land in their inbox.

How it can hurt you

  • Your name on someone else's content. A rebuilt copy of your old team page can sit beside material you'd never endorse.
  • Broken credibility. Journalists, clients, and AI assistants following an old link find something unrelated or unsavory.
  • Account takeover. Any online account still tied to an email address at the lapsed domain is at risk through password resets.
  • Confused entities. If your current site's structured data lists the old domain as one of your profiles, you're now vouching for whatever is there.

Protect the domains you still own

  1. Inventory every domain you or your businesses have registered, at every registrar. Old registrar accounts tied to old emails are the usual blind spot.
  2. Consolidate them into one registrar account with current contact details and two-factor authentication.
  3. Turn on auto-renew and use a payment method that won't expire unnoticed.
  4. Decide what each domain should do. Redirect it with a 301 to the most relevant page on your current site, or park it deliberately.
  5. Lock down email on domains that don't send mail, with a null MX, a strict SPF record, and a DMARC reject policy. See locking down a domain that never sends email.
  6. Renew for several years at a time for domains with valuable history. The cost is trivial compared with the risk.

If a domain has already lapsed

  • Check its status. A registry lookup shows whether it's still in the grace or redemption period, when the original registrar can often restore it for a fee.
  • If it's been bought, check whether it's for sale. Sometimes buying it back is the cheapest fix.
  • Move accounts off it. Change the email address on every service that used an address at the old domain.
  • Remove it from your own sites. Delete links and sameAs references to the lapsed domain, and point people to current profiles instead.
  • Update the sources you can. Ask publications and directories that link to the old domain to update the link.
  • Watch for impersonation. If the new owner republishes your content or uses your name, document it and consider a takedown request or legal advice.

Plan for endings

When a company closes or a partnership ends, decide who keeps each domain and for how long. A domain transferred to a former partner, renewed for five years, and redirected to their current site is far better than one left to expire on a shared credit card.
