Service-area businesses on Google Business Profile

A plumber, a roofer, a mobile dog groomer, or a pest control company does its work at the customer's door. Google calls these service-area businesses, and it treats them differently from shops: no public street address, a list of areas served instead, and a stricter eye on the details that remain. Most of the trouble I see with these profiles comes from three fields, the address, the areas, and the phone number, so this guide works through each one.

Which kind of business you are

Google recognizes three setups. A storefront serves customers at its address. A service-area business goes to its customers and has no premises where customers are received. A hybrid does both: a showroom or office that customers visit during posted hours, plus crews that travel. A tile company with a showroom is a hybrid; an electrician working from a home office is a service-area business.

Pick the one that matches how customers meet you, because the rest of the profile follows from it. A home-based business that lists its house as a storefront invites strangers to the door and tells Google something it can check and will eventually doubt.

Remove the address when customers don't come to you

Google's service-area help page is direct about it: if you don't serve customers at your business address, remove the address from the profile and enter only your service area. The address still exists in Google's records for verification and for placing you on its internal map. It just stops showing to the public.

Two addresses never qualify. Google's guidelines rule out a rented mailing address where the business doesn't operate, and they rule out P.O. boxes and remote mailboxes. A service-area business can list a so-called virtual office only if staff are actually there during business hours. A coworking desk you visit once a month fails that test.

Choose service areas you can serve

A profile can hold up to 20 service areas, set as cities, postal codes, or other named areas. Google's own tip is that the whole area shouldn't stretch beyond about two hours of driving time from where the business is based. Treat that as an outer limit.

Service areas describe where you work, and they carry little weight in where the profile ranks. Map results still weigh the distance between the searcher and the business location Google holds on file, so a company based in one suburb won't start ranking across a whole metro by listing twenty towns. What wins the farther towns is the website: a useful page for each area where you have real jobs, real photos, and real reviews to show.

Use a phone number that belongs to this business

The guidelines ask for a number that connects to the individual business location, a local number in place of a central call-center line whenever possible, and a number under the business's direct control. Premium-rate numbers aren't allowed at all.

This is where I find the quietest damage. On a recent competitor review for a home-services company in the Southeast, the client's profile showed a toll-free number while its website showed a local one with a different area code. Each number on its own was legitimate. Together they gave Google, directories, and AI tools two answers to the simplest question about the business. Pick one primary number, put it on the profile, the website header, and the contact page, and keep tracking numbers out of the primary slot.

Keep the name clean

Service-area businesses are tempted to add the city or the service to their name: "Smith Plumbing Denver Emergency Drain Cleaning." Google's guidelines say plainly that unnecessary information in the business name isn't permitted and can lead to suspension. The name on the profile should match the name on the truck, the invoice, and the state registration.

Verification is the gate

Until a profile is verified, nobody can change it, owner or manager. I recently worked on a service-area profile that showed in public with mismatched details, and because it had never been verified, nobody could correct them. If your profile is in that state, verification comes before every other fix. What competitors' listings tell you

Look up the businesses that outrank you and note what they show. In that same review, all nine competing listings in the client's market displayed a street address while the client's showed only a service area. The client shouldn't add one to match. Those competitors had set themselves up as storefronts or hybrids, and a business without premises for customers has no address to show. A true service-area business competes on what it can prove: reviews that mention the towns and jobs, photos from real sites, prompt answers to questions, and location pages that read like the work of someone who has been there.

A short checklist

  • Business type matches how customers meet you: storefront, service area, or hybrid.
  • Address removed from public view if customers never come to it; no mailboxes or unstaffed offices.
  • Service areas limited to places you serve, within about two hours of your base.
  • One primary local number, the same on the profile and the website.
  • Business name exactly as it appears on signage and paperwork.
  • Profile verified, with at least two people holding access.

Questions service businesses ask

Will removing my address hurt my rankings?

Google still knows where the business is based and uses that location. Removing the address from public view follows Google's guidance for businesses that don't receive customers, and it protects the privacy of a home address.

Can I have a profile in every town I serve?

No. One business gets one profile per real location where it operates. Extra profiles at virtual addresses are a common cause of suspensions.

Should my website list my address?

If you work from home, list the areas you serve and your phone number instead. Your legal notices and invoices can carry the mailing address.
