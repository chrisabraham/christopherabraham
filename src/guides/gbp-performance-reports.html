Reading the new Google Business Profile performance reports

Google is testing a rebuilt Performance section inside Google Business Profile, with an Overview that leads with recommendations and separate sections for Engagement, Reviews, Audience, and Search trends. The test arrived about a day after Google launched its new Business Home portal, and it changes what an owner should look at first. I covered the announcement in my weblog post on the rebuilt scoreboard and promised a full guide to reading the new reports. This is that guide, section by section, in the order I read them myself.

First, check whether you have it

Open your Business Profile and choose Performance. If the page opens with recommendations and the five new section names, your account is in the test and everything below applies to the screens in front of you. If it still shows the familiar single page of totals and charts, you are on the old reports and nothing is wrong with your account.

The rollout is gradual and account by account. Search Engine Roundtable reported the test on October 23, 2026, after Jaikishan Raturi of Wisevu and Syed Amir shared screenshots and video of their accounts, and noted that Barry Schwartz did not yet have it in his own account that day. There is no setting to request and no form to submit. While you wait, capture one screenshot of your old totals as a baseline. Old and new figures may not line up neatly, and a saved baseline settles arguments that memory always loses.

Overview: start with the recommendations

The Overview is the summary screen, and its most useful addition sits at the top: a short list of recommendations drawn from your profile's completeness and recent performance. Read that list before any chart on the page. It is Google's own prioritised worklist for this profile, and it tells you what the platform currently thinks is unfinished, stale, or worth confirming.

A new recommendation usually means one of two things: Google changed what it wants from profiles, or your details drifted out of date as hours, services, and attributes changed in the real business. A recommendation that disappears means the gap it named has closed. Work the list from the top, but verify every item against the business itself before accepting it. A recommendation is a prompt, not an order, and the owner who knows the shop floor outranks the algorithm's guess. Where a suggestion is wrong for your business, decline it and move on with a clear conscience.

Engagement: actions, counted honestly

Engagement gathers the actions people took from your profile: the calls, messages, direction requests, website visits, and bookings your listing produced. Look first at the direction of the totals across a whole quarter rather than any single week. Local demand moves with weather, holidays, school terms, and road works, and a quarter smooths most of that noise into a line you can actually read.

A rise means more searchers reached the point of acting, which is the only point that pays. A fall means less until you have run the interpretation check two sections below, because some of those actions now happen somewhere else. Before blaming the market, compare each action against the channel that receives it. If calls fall, ring the number on your own profile and confirm it connects to the right desk. Broken plumbing hides inside report totals far more often than lost demand does, and it is cheaper to fix.

Reviews: read below the star rating

The Reviews section breaks the detail out instead of compressing everything into one average: how many reviews arrived, how the ratings spread, and the words themselves, close enough to read in place. Look first at the distribution and the pace. Ten reviews carrying two furious outliers tell a different story than a smooth average suggests, and a steady trickle of recent reviews outweighs a proud total gathered years ago.

Then read the text as field research. A cluster of new reviews after a menu change, a hire, or a remodel is your customers writing the changelog. Phrases that repeat across reviews are the phrases the next searcher will meet, so note them exactly. Answer every review briefly and without argument, especially the unfair ones, because the reply is written for the reader who comes after. Then fix the operational thing the complaints repeat. Owners tend to read reviews as reputation. Read them as the cheapest customer research you will ever be handed.

Audience: who is finding you

Audience gathers the people behind the views into one place, so you can see who the profile reaches instead of guessing from the totals. Look first for a match or a mismatch: whether the audience arriving looks like the customers you actually want to serve. A profile can be busy and wrong at the same time, and busy wrongness still costs you the hours spent answering it.

A widening audience after a new service, a move, or a name change usually means the profile started matching new searches, which is growth you can plan around. A narrowing one can mean a competitor opened closer to your best customers, which is a different problem entirely. Hold the audience picture next to your service list and your service area. If strangers keep arriving for something you do not sell, your categories or services are promising it on your behalf. Correct the listing before you correct the customers.

Search trends: the customer's own words

Search trends shows the searches that led people to your profile and how those searches are moving over time. Look first at rising terms you did not plan for, and at your core money terms if they start slipping down the list. Both ends of that movement are instructions, one about what to add and one about what to defend.

Searchers rename things before businesses do. When a trade term gives way to a plain phrase, the market is rewriting your signage in its own vocabulary, and the profile that answers in that vocabulary wins the next visit. Mirror the winning words in your services, your business description, and the local pages of your website, spelled the way customers spell them. Retire the phrases nobody types anymore, however fond the founder remains of them. Sentiment is not a search strategy.

Falling interactions are not falling demand

The old reports trained owners to treat every interaction as demand arriving at the door. That link is weakening. AI answers now resolve questions that used to end with a tap on your profile: the hours, the price range, whether you stock the thing, whether people rate you well. The customer still buys. The tap just happens inside the answer instead, or never happens at all.

An October 2026 analysis of three American florists found profile interactions down 26 percent year over year, with website clicks down 30 percent, calls down 21 percent, and direction requests down 15 percent, while the shops traded on. Three shops make a small sample, so treat those figures as a direction rather than a measurement of your market. Still, the direction matches what I see across accounts: when interactions fall while revenue holds, demand did not vanish. The answer moved upstream. Your job shifts from winning the tap to being the business the answer names, which is why I track AI share of voice alongside these reports every month. Never judge a month by interactions alone again.

A monthly routine that takes twenty minutes

Pick one morning a month and give the reports twenty honest minutes, in this order. First, the Overview, clearing or consciously declining each recommendation. Second, Engagement, comparing the month with the month before and with the same month last year. Third, Reviews: the count, the average, the pace, and the three phrases customers repeated most. Fourth, Audience, in a single line about whether the people arriving are the people you want. Fifth, Search trends, choosing one rising term to adopt and one fading term to retire.

Write those five lines down in the same place every month. Reports reward memory, and a profile read monthly for a year teaches more than any single deep dive performed in a panic. End the routine by choosing exactly one action for the month ahead. A reading that produces nine worries produces none of the fixes, and the reports will be there next month to grade the one you picked.

Mistakes owners make with the old reports

Most of these will survive the redesign, so retire them now. Judging by days, and reading one rainy Tuesday as a trend. Comparing December with January and calling the difference decline. Chasing views, and celebrating impressions while calls and bookings quietly thin out. Treating a drop in calls as lost customers before checking the AI answers, the phone number, and the calendar for an explanation. Never capturing a baseline, so every redesign leaves the owner arguing with a memory. And reading the reports only when something already feels wrong, which guarantees the numbers arrive attached to a bad mood and get believed for the wrong reasons.
